Feasibility

A small prototype is achievable. A sustainable service still needs evidence, reviewed content, reliable tutors, and institutional support.

Prototype-stage assessment: the updated survey shows interest, but no live pilot, institutional partnership, confirmed budget, or operating service is established.

“Feasibility analysis is the process of determining if a business idea is viable.”

(Barringer & Ireland, 2019, Ch. 3, p. 109)

Our concept

Quizzness would connect course games, feedback and screened peer tutoring. Initial testing would focus on selected courses at a willing educational institution, with expansion guided by feedback and delivery capacity.

Product and service feasibility

The current prototype uses HTML, CSS and JavaScript. Puzzle Temple teaches percentages through matching fractions and ordering calculations, with feedback and visit-only XP. Its leaderboard is a fixed fictional table. The tutoring page demonstrates a sample request and application.

There is no working account system, backend, saved progress, real booking or tutor-management service. The implementation follows the assignment's web-design approach (Robbins, 2018, Chapters 4–9, 11–17 and 21–22).

In the updated survey, 23 of 30 respondents (76.7%) said they would definitely or probably practise more if a subject felt more game-like: 15 selected definitely and 8 probably. This supports testing the idea; it does not prove better learning outcomes.

Industry and target-market feasibility

Students seeking extra practice and peer tutors are the intended users. Universities, colleges and other educational institutions are potential partners and paying customers, with responsibilities for content approval, access, tutor eligibility and GiveBack recognition.

The survey covers CSEC/CXC, CAPE/Sixth Form, associate, bachelor's, master's, technical/vocational and other education levels. Nineteen of 30 respondents (63.3%) reported a bachelor's degree. The sample is not exclusively university-level or representative of all students.

Twenty-six respondents (86.7%) rated their likelihood of trying the combined concept 4 or 5 out of 5. Twenty (66.7%) said yes to tutor or strong-student help, and eight (26.7%) said maybe. Registration, return use, institutional demand and willingness to pay remain untested.

Evidence from the updated survey

The updated group report summarises 30 anonymous responses collected on 8–10 October 2026. Respondents could choose several study challenges, so these percentages do not total 100%.

These findings, together with group members' firsthand experience, support exploring extra course practice, useful feedback and access to peer support. They are reported experiences, rather than direct observations of study behaviour.

The sample does not establish market size, learning gains, repeat use or a paying customer. The recruitment method and response-verification process still need documenting. See all updated figures supplied in the report.

Organisational feasibility and operational risks

The team combines project management, development, business analysis, research, branding and marketing. No educational-institution partnership is confirmed. Course access, content verification, tutor training, coordination, user support and a safety/complaints process still need arrangements.

Prototype funding would use bootstrapping and sweat equity: team labour and limited, voluntary personal contributions, agreed and recorded by the group. No prices, budget totals or external financing are confirmed.

These are delivery assumptions to test, not evidence that an operating team or university process is already in place.

What could prevent a reliable pilot?
Risk or assumption Proposed response
Incorrect course content Seek a subject reviewer, check questions and explanations before use, and provide a way to report errors in a future pilot.
Insufficient tutor time or quality Agree screening, training, availability, and coordinator responsibilities. Reduce the pilot scope if support cannot be provided reliably.
GiveBack recognition is not agreed Seek a formal university agreement. Keep pending session time separate from approved records and make no recognition promise before approval.
Coordination workload is too high Identify who reviews content, handles session concerns, and verifies time. Record the workload before increasing participant numbers.
Privacy or consent arrangements are unclear Agree consent, minimal data needs, access responsibilities, and participant support before any live pilot. This prototype does not collect real personal data.
Costs exceed available funding Estimate content, development, supervision, hosting, and support costs; test institutional willingness to pay before assuming a viable licence.

Organisational and financial readiness

The group has named project roles, but content review, tutor oversight, and ongoing service capacity still need agreement. Financial feasibility is not yet established: a realistic budget and evidence of a paying customer are missing.

Prototype testing and next steps

The updated plan is to find a suitable partner, invite students and tutors to test the prototype, and improve it using their feedback. No live pilot results are claimed. Participant numbers, duration and delivery targets have not been fixed in the updated report.

What testing needs to establish
Area Proposed check
Practice and feedback Observe whether students can complete activities and understand why their answers are right or wrong.
Peer-support journey Check whether students can find the request form and understand the difference between a demonstration and a real booking.
Tutor delivery Review tutor screening, training, session coordination, student support and complaints arrangements.
Continued use Assess whether participants choose to return for practice after trying the prototype.
Institutional fit Agree course permissions, content review, responsibilities and GiveBack requirements with a willing institution.
Cost and capacity Estimate development, hosting, reviewed content and support costs before committing to a larger service.

Our judgement

A small prototype is achievable with the team's current skills. A working service still depends on reviewed content, reliable tutors, institutional cooperation and an affordable operating plan. Expand only after these requirements have been tested.
