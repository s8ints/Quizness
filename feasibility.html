Evidence, assumptions, and practical delivery

Feasibility

A small prototype is achievable. A sustainable service still needs evidence, reviewed content, reliable tutors, and institutional support.

Early-stage assessment: the survey is exploratory, and interviews, a live pilot, institutional approval, and financial viability have not been established.

“Feasibility analysis is the process of determining if a business idea is viable.”

(Barringer & Ireland, 2019, Ch. 3, p. 109)

Our concept

Quizzness would connect course games, feedback and screened peer tutoring. We would start at one university with Programming and Mathematics, then consider more courses if the pilot works.

Product and service feasibility

Students would get extra practice outside class and a way to ask for help with a specific topic. Our prototype shows the basic journey, but it does not prove that students would return or improve their results.

Reusable activity templates could reduce development work. Course review, tutor screening and session coordination would still take time and need university support.

Industry and target-market feasibility

Our first users would be university students and peer tutors. Universities and colleges would be the main paying customers. We need to check their budgets, willingness to participate and rules for GiveBack recognition.

Existing study tools show that digital practice is already available. Our proposed difference is the link from a weak course topic to screened peer help. We have not tested whether that difference is enough to attract users.

Evidence so far

Retrieval Practice research guidance supports recalling information as a learning strategy. Duolingo's teaching-method overview provides an example of repeated practice and game elements. Neither source proves demand for Quizzness.

Our survey of 28 students (8–9 October 2026) gives early evidence of need: 22 (78.6%) have difficulty understanding course topics at least sometimes, 19 (67.9%) would want help from a tutor or a strong student, and 24 (85.7%) rated their likelihood of trying the platform 4 or 5 out of 5. These are stated intentions from a small sample, not measured use.

We have not completed interviews or a pilot. University interest still needs evidence.

What the survey cannot establish

These responses do not establish repeat use, improved learning, willingness to pay, or demand across all students. The recruitment method and questionnaire need to accompany the figures before wider conclusions are drawn.

Evidence placeholder: group to supply the survey questions, recruitment method, and anonymised aggregate results. No respondent information should be collected through this prototype.

Operational risks and proposed mitigations

These are delivery assumptions to test, not evidence that an operating team or university process is already in place.

What could prevent a reliable pilot?
Risk or assumptionProposed response
Incorrect course contentSeek a subject reviewer, check questions and explanations before use, and provide a way to report errors in a future pilot.
Insufficient tutor time or qualityAgree screening, training, availability, and coordinator responsibilities. Reduce the pilot scope if support cannot be provided reliably.
GiveBack recognition is not agreedSeek a formal university agreement. Keep pending session time separate from approved records and make no recognition promise before approval.
Coordination workload is too highIdentify who reviews content, handles session concerns, and verifies time. Record the workload before increasing participant numbers.
Privacy or consent arrangements are unclearAgree consent, minimal data needs, access responsibilities, and participant support before any live pilot. This prototype does not collect real personal data.
Costs exceed available fundingEstimate content, development, supervision, hosting, and support costs; test institutional willingness to pay before assuming a viable licence.

Organisational and financial readiness

The group has named project roles, but content review, tutor oversight, and ongoing service capacity still need agreement. Financial feasibility is not yet established: a realistic budget and evidence of a paying customer are missing.

Proposed pilot evaluation

Our planning targets are a four-week pilot with 50 to 100 students, 10 approved tutors and 20 to 30 activities per course. These numbers are targets, not results.

No pilot results are claimed; these are proposed test conditions only. The present demonstration covers one Mathematics topic. A two-course pilot would require more reviewed content and agreed delivery capacity.

What we need to test
Question Proposed test
Do students need this? Interview 12 learners and 3 tutors about practice and finding help.
Can students follow the website? Ask 5 learners to try the practice-to-tutoring journey. Aim for at least 4 to finish without help.
Are the activities useful and correct? Have a coordinator review the content and compare separate tasks before and after practice.
Can tutoring be organised? Check tutor availability, student feedback and the time needed to manage sessions.
Will students return? With consent, record pilot participation. Our initial target is for at least half to return in week two.
Can the programme be funded and recognised? Agree costs, responsibilities and GiveBack rules with a university partner.

Decision after evaluation

Review the findings with a prospective partner before expanding. Revise or pause if content cannot be reviewed, tutor support is unreliable, consent arrangements remain unresolved, or delivery costs cannot be supported.

Our judgement

A small prototype is achievable. A working service depends on approved content, reliable tutors, university cooperation and enough repeat use to cover its costs. We should test these assumptions before expanding.

This analysis uses the product/service and industry/target-market categories from Barringer and Ireland (2019, Ch. 3, pp. 109–121).
