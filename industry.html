Market & Launch Strategy

Start with a willing educational partner, test the learning-to-help journey, and expand only if the evidence supports it.

Proposed strategy, not business activity: no pilot has launched, no recruitment channel is agreed, and no institutional customer or partner is confirmed. All targets below are planning examples.

Target audience and potential customers

The first intended market is students in selected, willing universities or other educational institutions who want additional course practice and a route to peer assistance. Peer tutors would support learning and could work toward agreed rewards or recognised GiveBack hours.

Educational institutions are the proposed partners and primary paying customers. They would help approve content, arrange access and permissions, screen tutors and confirm GiveBack requirements. No partnership is confirmed.

The survey includes several education levels: 19 of 30 respondents (63.3%) reported a bachelor's degree. It indicates interest but does not establish market size or a confirmed buyer.

Our proposed difference

Quizzness combines gamified course practice, immediate feedback explaining mistakes, and a direct route to screened peer support. The focus is on course material supplied or approved by lecturers, teachers and other educators.

A positioning statement to test is: “Quizzness provides students with gamified course practice, immediate feedback to understand mistakes, and direct access to peer support in one student-first experience.”

Try the practice activities and the tutoring demonstration. These show part of the intended service rather than a complete operating platform.

The updated launch plan

  1. Build and test the prototype

    Use an initial course-practice and peer-support prototype for trial and error.

  2. Find a partner

    Seek a business or educational-institution partner that can support the venture's development.

  3. Invite students and tutors to test it

    Gather feedback on the activities, explanations and route to peer support.

  4. Develop from the feedback

    Use what participants report and demonstrate to guide more detailed development.

  5. Arrange permissions

    Obtain the licences, course-content permissions and institutional agreements needed for a live service.

  6. Agree the budget and safety arrangements

    Confirm responsibilities, data protection, tutor policies and support before accepting real users or bookings.

The updated report does not set participant counts, a pilot duration or a launch date. No live pilot has taken place.

Marketing approach and recruitment

Our proposed message is: practise a difficult course topic, understand the feedback, and find peer support when needed. Recruitment should show this journey honestly, rather than promise better grades or recognised hours.

Product

A focused course-support pilot combining short activities, explanations, retries, and screened peer help. Show the actual demonstration before asking students to participate.

Price

Institutional licensing is the proposed primary model. Pilot access terms and any charges would need agreement; no price is confirmed. See the pricing placeholders.

Place

Begin within the approved courses and student-support setting at one university. The school prototype opens locally; a future live distribution method would need a separate decision.

Promotion

Consider permission-based lecturer announcements, student-association introductions, campus notices, and short demonstrations. None of these channels is currently agreed.

Possible early recruitment channels

  • Course coordinators: request permission for a short introduction to students in the selected courses.
  • Student-support services: discuss how to reach learners seeking extra practice without implying endorsement.
  • Student associations and study groups: ask organisers to share a voluntary invitation through approved channels.
  • On-campus demonstrations: let interested students try the critical flow and explain the pilot's limitations.

Ask future participants how they heard about the pilot to assess which channels reach suitable learners. Recruitment should be voluntary and permission-based; do not collect real personal details through this prototype.

What testing should show

Evaluate whether students understand the activity feedback, complete the practice-to-support journey and choose to return. Assess reviewed-content workload, tutor availability, session coordination and the cost of continuing.

Institutional interest, suitable course permissions, confirmed responsibilities and an affordable delivery plan would also be needed. The updated report supplies no numerical success thresholds or achieved results.

Launch risks and assumptions

  • Permission and demand: access to a campus or course is not guaranteed, and survey interest may not translate into use.
  • Delivery capacity: preparing reviewed activities and finding trained tutors could limit the proposed scale.
  • Marketing fit: approved channels may attract too few participants or an unrepresentative group.
  • Recognition and funding: GiveBack approval, responsibilities, and budgets must be agreed before making commitments.

See the operational risks and mitigations and the sustainability questions.

Industry trends and existing study habits

The updated report identifies gamification, digital study tools and peer learning as relevant trends. Sailer and Homner (2020) examine gamification in education; Bengesai, Amusa and Dhunpath (2023) examine formal peer learning in higher education. These sources do not establish outcomes for Quizzness.

In the 30-response survey, existing study tools included class notes (23; 76.7%), AI tools (20; 66.7%), practice questions (19; 63.3%) and YouTube videos (17; 56.7%). Respondents could choose more than one tool.

For proposed game features, 17 respondents (56.7%) selected achievements or badges, 15 (50.0%) missions or quests, and 12 each (40.0%) XP/levels and progress. These findings guide prototype choices rather than guarantee adoption.

Other alternatives

WhatsApp and study groups already offer familiar peer communication. Quizzness would need to make help focused and reliable enough to be useful alongside them. Quizlet's flashcards, quizzes and practice tools mean Quizzness needs more than quizzes alone: course relevance, useful feedback and peer support are central to the proposal.

Porter's Five Forces

These are the updated group's initial judgements using the industry-analysis framework in Barringer and Ireland (2019, Chapter 5).

Threat of substitutes: moderate

Free notes, YouTube, AI tools, study groups and existing study products are available. Quizzness is intended to complement these resources.

Threat of new entrants: moderate to low

Basic quiz interfaces can be copied. Trusted institutional relationships, approved content and tutor-screening arrangements would be harder to reproduce once established; Quizzness has not yet secured these advantages.

Rivalry among existing firms

The proposed focus is a narrower combination of course-specific practice and peer support. Whether that difference is valuable enough needs testing.

Supplier power: moderate

The service would depend on institutions, educators, content reviewers and tutors for permissions, expertise and supervision.

Buyer power: moderate to high

Students have free alternatives, and institutions can compare existing services before committing resources. Educational value, ease of use, delivery cost and long-term usefulness need evidence.
