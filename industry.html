Market & Launch Strategy

Start with a focused university pilot, test the learning-to-help journey, and expand only if the evidence supports it.

Proposed strategy, not business activity: no pilot has launched, no recruitment channel is agreed, and no institutional customer or partner is confirmed. All targets below are planning examples.

Target audience and potential customers

Students and peer tutors

Our initial users would be university students who need extra course practice or explanations. Students with strong subject knowledge could apply to become peer tutors, subject to screening and training.

Universities and colleges

Institutions would be the proposed primary paying customers. Course coordinators and student-support staff would need to assess content quality, workload, funding, and tutor oversight before adoption.

A focused first market

We propose starting at one university with Mathematics and Programming, rather than offering every subject immediately. UWI Cave Hill is a possible setting to explore, not a confirmed customer or pilot partner.

The current prototype demonstrates Mathematics only; Programming remains a labelled preview. A two-course pilot would require additional reviewed content before recruitment.

Market information placeholder: group to confirm the first institution, course codes, eligible cohort size, decision-makers, and evidence of institutional interest. No local market-size estimate is claimed.

Our proposed competitive advantage

Quizzness would connect course-specific practice and explanations to screened peer help and reviewed service-time records. The value proposition is a clearer next step after a student makes a mistake, rather than game elements alone.

This positioning is a hypothesis. We need to test whether it reduces the effort of finding useful help and gives students a reason to return, compared with existing study tools or familiar study groups.

Proposed pilot launch

Our existing planning targets are a four-week pilot with 50 to 100 students, 10 approved tutors, and 20 to 30 activities per course. These are proposed test conditions, not recruited participants, completed content, or results.

  1. Prepare and agree the scope

    Seek institutional permission, identify course reviewers and a tutor coordinator, agree costs and consent arrangements, and review course material. GiveBack recognition must be agreed separately.

  2. Recruit a small, voluntary cohort

    Use approved course and student-support channels to invite learners and potential tutors. Explain what the pilot offers, what is being tested, and how feedback would be used.

  3. Run the four-week pilot

    Introduce the practice-to-help journey, offer reviewed activities and tutor support, and check participation, content issues, and coordination workload. This prototype itself does not run that pilot.

  4. Review before expanding

    Compare findings with the agreed measures and costs. Continue, revise, or pause the proposal before adding courses or approaching further institutions.

Launch planning placeholder: group to confirm dates, responsible people, permissions, content capacity, and a realistic pilot budget before treating these targets as commitments.

Marketing approach and recruitment

Our proposed message is: practise a difficult course topic, understand the feedback, and find peer support when needed. Recruitment should show this journey honestly, rather than promise better grades or recognised hours.

Product

A focused course-support pilot combining short activities, explanations, retries, and screened peer help. Show the actual demonstration before asking students to participate.

Price

Institutional licensing is the proposed primary model. Pilot access terms and any charges would need agreement; no price is confirmed. See the pricing placeholders.

Place

Begin within the approved courses and student-support setting at one university. The school prototype opens locally; a future live distribution method would need a separate decision.

Promotion

Consider permission-based lecturer announcements, student-association introductions, campus notices, and short demonstrations. None of these channels is currently agreed.

Possible early recruitment channels

  • Course coordinators: request permission for a short introduction to students in the selected courses.
  • Student-support services: discuss how to reach learners seeking extra practice without implying endorsement.
  • Student associations and study groups: ask organisers to share a voluntary invitation through approved channels.
  • On-campus demonstrations: let interested students try the critical flow and explain the pilot's limitations.

Ask future participants how they heard about the pilot to assess which channels reach suitable learners. Recruitment should be voluntary and permission-based; do not collect real personal details through this prototype.

Launch risks and assumptions

  • Permission and demand: access to a campus or course is not guaranteed, and survey interest may not translate into use.
  • Delivery capacity: preparing reviewed activities and finding trained tutors could limit the proposed scale.
  • Marketing fit: approved channels may attract too few participants or an unrepresentative group.
  • Recognition and funding: GiveBack approval, responsibilities, and budgets must be agreed before making commitments.

Industry trends

Gamification, digital study tools and peer learning are relevant to our idea. Duolingo, Quizlet and Kahoot! show different approaches to practice and engagement.

Our proposal cites a 2026 Research and Markets Gamification in Education Market Report; the group still needs to confirm the full report link and publication details. A meta-analysis of gamification in education found positive learning effects that varied between studies (Sailer & Homner, 2020).

Our own survey of 28 students (October 2026) points to a local need: 22 (78.6%) said they have difficulty understanding course topics at least sometimes, and 19 (67.9%) said they would want help from a tutor or a strong student. Only 8 (28.6%) currently use study groups. This is a small sample, so it does not describe all students.

These examples do not tell us the size of the local market. We still need research with university staff.

Porter's Five Forces

Using the industry-analysis framework in Barringer and Ireland (2019, Ch. 5), these are the questions we need to consider:

1. Substitutes

Could students get enough help from study groups or existing tools? They may not switch unless our feedback and tutor access are useful.

2. Rivalry

“In most industries, the major determinant of industry profitability is the level of competition among the firms already competing in the industry.”

(Barringer & Ireland, 2019, Ch. 5, p. 195)

How would we compete with other study tools? We would focus on course relevance and the connection to peer support.

3. Supplier power

How dependent would we be on lecturers, tutors and universities? Their time, course materials and approval would affect what we can offer.

4. Buyer power

What would universities and students pay for? Buyers could compare us with free or established alternatives and negotiate prices.

5. New entrants

Could another provider copy the idea? Practice interfaces may be easy to copy. Reliable university partnerships, reviewed content and recognised GiveBack processes could take longer to build.

These are initial judgements. Interviews and the pilot should help us check them.
