Barringer & Ireland · Chapter 5

Industry and competitors

Our proposed position is reviewed, course-specific application practice. We must test whether students value it enough to choose it over familiar alternatives.

Industry trends and their implications

Chapter 5 distinguishes environmental trends from business trends when assessing an industry. [1, pp. 189–191]

  • Material conversion: Quizlet offers AI-generated study guides and practice tests from uploaded material. Our inference: automatic quiz creation alone is weak differentiation. [3]
  • Independent game-based practice: Kahoot! offers self-study and solo experiences. Our inference: students can already practise without a live host, raising the standard for our experience. [4]
  • Validation and privacy: UNESCO calls for human-centred validation and data protection when using generative AI in education. If we adopt AI conversion, reviewed output and limited data collection would be part of our proposed delivery process. [5]

Porter’s Five Forces: initial assessment

The framework covers substitutes, new entrants, rivalry, supplier power and buyer power. The ratings below are preliminary venture judgements, not measured industry scores. [1, pp. 192–199]

Forces affecting the proposed course-practice service
ForceAssessment and proposed response
Substitutes · highAnki offers recall-based practice. [6] We would test whether course application and explanations offer enough additional value.
Rivalry · highQuizlet and Kahoot! already offer study activities. [3] [4] Start with a course niche instead of a broad quiz catalogue.
New entrants · moderate to highOur interface and templates could be imitated. Reviewed content and course partnerships would be proposed assets, not guaranteed barriers.
Supplier power · moderateOur proposed model depends on tutors, course-material permissions and qualified reviewers. Confirm permissions and review capacity before promising course coverage.
Buyer power · highStudents could choose alternatives, and an institutional buyer could decline or negotiate. Demonstrate usefulness and delivery costs in a limited pilot.

The four entry questions

Chapter 5 applies the forces through four questions about potential venture success. These are paraphrased below. [1, pp. 200–201, Figure 5.2]

  1. Is entry realistic? Conditionally, for a two-course pilot. Broad entry is less attractive given the alternatives above.
  2. Can we reduce the forces that limit profitability? Reusable templates and course partnerships might reduce delivery effort and substitution. Review costs and buyer pressure would remain.
  3. Is there a position that reduces those forces? We propose reviewed tertiary course practice connected to screened peers and approved service records. Whether this is underserved must be tested with students and lecturers.
  4. Is our model superior and difficult to copy? Not demonstrated. Licensing and optional tutoring commissions are familiar; defensibility would need to come from content quality and partner relationships.

Judgement: test a focused niche. Current evidence does not justify claiming a protected position or a disruptive advantage.
